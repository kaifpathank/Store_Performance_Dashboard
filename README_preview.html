Store Performance Executive Suite Dashboard

View the live dashboard in Google Sheets

An analytics dashboard that summarizes store-level performance across 898 store records, compares stores on sales, customer traffic and space efficiency, and flags stores that deserve a closer look.

Headline finding: daily customer traffic and store sales are almost completely uncorrelated across the chain (r = 0.009). Busy stores are not reliably the high-revenue stores, which shifts the question from "how do we get more footfall?" to "why does footfall convert so differently from store to store?"

Business Questions

  • What are the total and average sales across the chain?
  • Which stores have the highest and lowest sales?
  • How does daily customer traffic vary between stores?
  • Does higher customer traffic go with higher sales?
  • Which stores should be reviewed first?

Dataset

The dataset (Stores.csv) contains 898 store records with five fields:

Column Description
Store ID Store identifier
Store_Area Recorded store area
Items_Available Number of items available in the store
Daily_Customer_Count Recorded daily customer count
Store_Sales Recorded sales value

Key Insights

1. Foot traffic does not explain sales

The correlation between daily customer count and store sales is r = 0.009, effectively no linear relationship. High-traffic stores (1,000+ visitors/day) appear across the entire sales range, from roughly $20k to above $100k.

2. Top stores win on spend per customer, not volume

Four of the top five stores have fewer than 1,000 daily customers. Store #693 reaches $99,480 on only 480 daily customers ($207.25 per customer), against a chain figure of $75.49.

3. Store #610 is the top record

Store #610 has the highest sales in the dataset at $116,320, about 96% above the chain average. It also leads on space productivity ($58.48 per sq ft vs a $39.96 average). This identifies it as the record to study; the data does not show why it performs well.

4. A group of high-traffic stores is not converting

The bottom-right of the scatter plot holds stores with 1,000+ visitors/day and sales below $40k. These stores bring in traffic but earn little from it.

5. Store area and item count move almost in lockstep

Every store in the top-12 ledger carries about 1.2 items per sq ft, as does the chain average. Area and item count may carry nearly the same information, so they should not be treated as independent drivers without checking their correlation.

Recommendations

These are hypotheses to test, not conclusions the data proves.

  1. Review the high-traffic, low-sales stores first. Compare pricing, stock availability, staffing and layout against stores with similar traffic and higher sales.
  2. Study the high-spend, low-traffic stores (for example #693 and #433) to find what drives their sales per customer, and test whether it can be replicated.
  3. Stop using footfall alone as a performance signal. With r = 0.009, traffic cannot predict sales, so evaluate stores on sales per customer and sales per sq ft as well.
  4. Add more variables (location type, product mix, promotions) to explain the gap between traffic and revenue.

Data Quality Notes

  • Totals reconcile: $53,298,770 ÷ 898 stores = $59,352.75 average, and $53,298,770 ÷ 706,070 customers = $75.49 per customer.
  • Thresholds ($40k, $75k, 1,000 visitors/day) were chosen for exploration and are not company performance standards.

Limitations

  • Currency and area units follow the dashboard labels and are not confirmed by source documentation.
  • The correlation coefficient measures linear association only and does not establish causation.
  • Review thresholds are analyst-selected, not validated business categories.
  • The time period covered by sales and customer-count fields is not documented, which limits how sales per customer can be interpreted.
  • Formulas and report drafting were AI-assisted (Gemini).
